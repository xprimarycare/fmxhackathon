The premise

You already know what's broken. Share with everyone how you'd fix it.

This is a learning morning. You'll spend it building with AI tools — some of you for the first time — alongside colleagues who have already done it and can show you how. The prizes are real, but the point is to stretch: take a problem you've quietly wished someone would fix, and find out how far you can get with it before lunch.

Bring a problem you actually care about

Not the easiest one to solve — the one that's been bothering you for years. Think harder about why it's still broken than about what to build, and the building gets easier.

Learn the tools by using them

You don't need to write code. AI build tools have collapsed the distance between describing an idea and having a working version of it, and this is a supervised first run at that.

Colleagues who've already done it

Physician mentors on the floor all morning. They've built things, hit the walls you're about to hit, and can get you unstuck in two minutes rather than twenty.

Challenge briefs

Two tracks.

Pick one track during problem selection and stay in it — scoring happens within tracks, and there's a winner in each. Both briefs are deliberately wide. Your job between 8:30 and 9:00 is to make one of them narrow enough to build.

Track 01

Patient Empowerment

How might we help patients take a more active, informed, and confident role in their healthcare?

Patients are often expected to navigate complex medical information, fragmented systems, difficult decisions, and unclear next steps with limited support. This track challenges teams to design solutions that give patients greater knowledge, agency, access, and control throughout their healthcare journey.

Teams may address challenges such as

  • Understanding diagnoses, test results, medications, or treatment options
  • Preparing for appointments and asking better questions
  • Navigating referrals, insurance, costs, and follow-up care
  • Managing chronic conditions between visits
  • Accessing trustworthy and understandable health information
  • Communicating needs, goals, preferences, and symptoms
  • Reducing barriers related to language, health literacy, disability, or technology
  • Helping caregivers and families participate appropriately in care
What wins this trackThe strongest solutions will address a specific patient problem, fit naturally into patients' lives, and make healthcare easier to understand or navigate without creating additional burden.
Track 02

Clinical Team Empowerment

How might we help clinicians and care teams spend more time delivering excellent care and less time fighting the healthcare system?

Physicians and clinical teams regularly encounter administrative burden, fragmented information, inefficient workflows, communication failures, and repetitive manual work. This track challenges teams to create solutions that help healthcare professionals work more effectively, collaborate more easily, and focus their attention on the work that matters most.

Teams may address challenges such as

  • Reducing documentation and administrative work
  • Improving clinical decision support without creating alert fatigue
  • Making relevant patient information easier to find and act upon
  • Improving communication between clinicians, staff, patients, and outside organizations
  • Simplifying referrals, prior authorizations, results management, or care coordination
  • Supporting quality improvement, peer learning, or clinical feedback
  • Reducing cognitive overload, burnout, and unnecessary interruptions
  • Improving team-based care and ensuring work reaches the right person
  • Helping clinicians identify and address gaps in care
What wins this trackThe strongest solutions will solve a clearly defined problem experienced by real clinical teams, integrate realistically into healthcare workflows, and improve care without simply adding another task, alert, or dashboard.
Cross-track note

Plenty of good ideas serve both your patients and your team. Declare the track whose primary user you're designing for — the one whose day gets measurably better — and weigh your tradeoffs from there. If you're genuinely torn, pick the one where you can point to a specific person you know.

Submission instructions

Submit a three-minute video.

Every team submits one recorded video overview of what they built, with a shareable link, plus any supplemental files. The submission window is 11:30 – 12:00.

Recording

Use a free screen-recording tool.

Both of these are free, run in the browser, need no editing skills, and give you a shareable link the moment you stop recording. If you already have a tool you like, use that instead — we only care that the link opens.

Loom →

Records your screen and your face at once. Generous free tier, instant link, and viewers don't need an account. The safe default if you've never done this.

Tella →

Slightly more polished output — clean backgrounds, easy trimming, and you can re-record a single section without starting over. Worth it if you want to take a second take.

Best approach

Narrate over whatever you built while you click through it, or narrate over a short slide deck — both work, and neither is penalised. Scoring is about how clearly the concept lands, not how finished it looks. Just make sure there's something on screen rather than only a talking head.

Structure

What to cover in three minutes.

Not a rule, but it helps guide you to include the essential information in the right sequence.

0:00 – 0:30

The problem

One specific clinical moment that goes badly today. Name the patient or the clinician. If a colleague wouldn't nod within fifteen seconds, it's too abstract.

0:30 – 2:00

The concept

The bulk of your time. Walk through what you built — prototype, mockup, or slides — and narrate how it would work. Show the one core interaction rather than touring every screen.

2:00 – 2:30

Adoption

Who it's for, who would champion it inside a practice, and how it realistically gets picked up — including who would pay for it and out of which budget.

2:30 – 3:00

Barriers and what's next

What would stop this from being adopted if it existed. Say plainly which parts are illustrative. Then one sentence on what you'd do next.

Required

What the form asks for.

Team & track

Team name, every member's name and specialty, and your chosen track. List everyone — prizes follow this list.

Video link

A shareable Loom, Tella, or equivalent URL. Three minutes maximum.

Problem statement — 50 words max

One paragraph naming the specific user and the specific moment that goes wrong today. This is read first, before your video plays.

Supplemental files — optional

Anything that helps convey what you built:

  • A live link to the prototype, or a repository
  • Your slide deck
  • Screenshots, workflow diagrams, or design files
  • Notes from anyone you tested it on during the morning

Supplemental files are reviewed but never a substitute for the video.

Share your tech stack

List the models, tools, and templates you used.

Check your link before you submit

The single most common way to lose a morning's work: a video set to "only people in my organization." Open your own link in a private browsing window first. If it asks you to sign in, your video can't be reviewed.

Judging

Scoring rubric.

Five criteria, 20 points each, 100 total.

Judged on the concept, not the polish

You have two and a half hours. Nobody expects a finished product, and scoring is not about how sophisticated the build is. What matters is whether the concept comes across clearly — what the problem is, who it's for, and whether you've thought about what it would take for it to exist. A rough prototype with a sharp, well-reasoned idea beats a slick one you can't explain.

Mentors

Physicians who build things.

Every mentor is a physician with experience in health technology. They understand how healthcare works, which means you can skip the background and get straight to the part you're stuck on.

They circulate the room for the whole 9:00–11:30 work session. Two checkpoints are suggested on the schedule, but flag anyone down at any time — that is the entire reason they're here.

How to use a mentor well

Open with your problem statement and one specific question. "Would you use this?" gets you a collegial yes. "What would make you close this and go back to what you were doing?" gets you something you can act on before 11:00.

    Building without a tech background

    5
    I'm not an engineer. Can I actually build something in one morning?

    Yes — and this event is designed on that assumption. Nobody in the room is a professional software developer, so nobody is starting at a disadvantage.

    What makes this possible now is that AI build tools have collapsed the distance between describing a thing and having a working version of it. People who have never written a line of code routinely produce a functioning prototype in an afternoon. Two and a half hours is tight but real.

    And the thing you bring that can't be taught in a morning — knowing which problem is actually worth solving — is worth 40 of the 100 rubric points.

    Do I need to know how to code?

    No. Teams win on the clarity of the problem and the sense of the solution, not on implementation. Perfectly valid ways to build:

    • AI build tools that generate a working app from a description
    • No-code and low-code builders for forms, workflows, and simple apps
    • Clickable design prototypes — Figma or similar, where nothing is real but every click works
    • A narrated slide walkthrough of the interaction, if that's genuinely the best way to show it

    The last one scores lowest on Prototype & video execution, so use it as a fallback rather than a plan. Mentors are here specifically to get you past whichever part you haven't done before.

    What should I set up before October 20?

    Half an hour the night before is worth an hour on the day:

    • Create a free Loom or Tella account and record a ten-second test video. Confirm you can open the link in a private browsing window.
    • Make sure your laptop can screen-share and that your microphone works.
    • If you already use an AI build tool, log in and confirm you have credits.
    • Think about one problem. Not a solution — a problem.
    Does it have to be software?

    No. A workflow redesign, a communication protocol, a new form, a service change — all valid, as long as your video shows it working in some form. A role-played patient conversation or an annotated workflow with a live walkthrough can clear the bar. What doesn't clear it is describing what you would have built.

    What if we don't finish?

    Submit anyway. A sharply framed problem with a half-working prototype and an honest account of what broke beats several polished entries that never named a real patient. Nearly every team feels unfinished at 11:30 — that's the format, not a failure.

    Teams

    4
    How do we form teams?

    Team formation runs 8:30 – 9:00, immediately after the keynote.

    Write your problem on a card in one sentence and post it on the idea wall, or read what's already up there and go stand next to the one you'd want to fix. With 100 participants in the room, someone else has your problem.

    How big can a team be?

    Five participants per team. Big enough to split the work across a two-and-a-half-hour build, small enough that nobody spends the morning waiting on a decision.

    With 100 participants, that makes 20 teams.

    Do I need to know which track I'm in before I arrive?

    No. Choose during problem selection and declare it on the submission form. If your idea spans both, pick the track whose primary user your solution serves. You can switch until you submit, but do it early — there's a winner in each track, and switching at 11:45 means you framed for the wrong audience all morning.

    Can one team submit to both tracks?

    No — one submission, one track, per team. Splitting your attention across two briefs in two and a half hours is the fastest way to place in neither.

    Projects & eligibility

    4
    Do solutions have to be built during the hackathon, or can I showcase an existing project?

    They have to be built during the hackathon. Existing projects are not eligible for judging — this is a build event, not a demo day, and showcasing prior work isn't fair to teams starting from nothing at 9:00.

    What you can bring:

    • Generic scaffolding — starter templates, design systems, an AI tool you already pay for.
    • Ideas and research. Thinking hard about a problem in advance is encouraged. Show up with a strong problem statement.

    What you can't bring: a working prototype of your solution, problem-specific work done before the event, or a product you're already using in practice. The idea and everything that demonstrates it must be built between 8:00 and 12:00 on October 20.

    Can I keep working on something I started at a previous event?

    Not as a competing entry. You're welcome to attend and keep building, and mentors will happily give you feedback — but a continuation of prior work can't be scored against teams that started this morning. The better move is to take what you learned and attack a different slice of the same problem from scratch.

    What if my idea is something a vendor already sells?

    Then you've found a real problem, which is most of the battle. Say so in your video and explain why the existing product doesn't work in your setting — too expensive, wrong workflow, not available on your EHR, ignored by your team. "The thing exists but nobody can actually use it" is a legitimate and often excellent problem statement.

    How finished does it need to be?

    Much less finished than you think, and much more working than you think. One narrow interaction running end to end beats five features that are all 60% done.

    Mocking parts is completely acceptable — hardcoded data, a fake API response, a human behind the curtain. Just say which parts are real in your video. That candour is rewarded; overclaiming is not.

    Submitting your video

    6
    Are there live demos?

    No. Every team submits a recorded three-minute video between 11:30 and 12:00.

    Submissions are reviewed after the morning ends. Winners are announced Thursday, October 22, 3:30 – 5:00 PM at the Main Stage Event in the Karl Dean Ballroom.

    What should the video actually look like?

    Narrate over your prototype while you click through it, or narrate over a short slide deck. Screen recording with your voice on top — that's it. No editing, no music, no title cards.

    What if we run over three minutes?

    We may not count any content beyond three minutes as part of the evaluation, so lead with what matters most.

    Do we all have to be on camera?

    Not required — but please do. Part of what makes these videos worth watching is seeing the people behind the ideas, and both Loom and Tella put your face in a small corner bubble while your screen fills the rest, so it costs you nothing.

    It doesn't affect your score either way. One narrator is usually cleaner than four, so if the whole team wants to appear, a quick round of introductions at the start works nicely.

    Do I have to use Loom or Tella?

    No — any tool that produces a shareable link works. We recommend those two because they're free, browser-based, and generate a link the moment you stop recording. Whatever you use, the requirement is the same: the link must open without signing in.

    Test it in a private browsing window before you submit. A link that asks for a login scores zero.

    What counts as a supplemental file?

    Anything that helps convey what you built: a live link to the prototype, a repository, your slide deck, screenshots, workflow diagrams, or notes from someone you tested it on. All optional.

    Supplemental files are reviewed but never a substitute for the video. Assume the three minutes are all that gets watched.

    Logistics

    What should I bring?
    • A laptop and charger — you can't build or record on a phone
    • Headphones with a microphone, which make your video noticeably clearer in a room of 100 people
    • Any AI tool accounts you'll want, set up the night before

    Come anyway. But the 8:00 keynote and the 8:30 team formation block are where teams and problems get set, so arriving after 9:00 makes it hard to join a team and very hard to submit competitively.

    Can I attend remotely?

    This is an in-person event. The mentor access and the hallway conversations are most of the value, and a four-hour format doesn't leave room for hybrid coordination. Plan to be in the room from 8:00 to 12:00.

    Do I have to come back for the Main Stage Event?

    Winning doesn't depend on attending. But awards and closing remarks run Thursday, October 22, 3:30 – 5:00 PM in the Karl Dean Ballroom, the winning videos get shown, and it's where the conversations about actually building these things happen. Come back.
